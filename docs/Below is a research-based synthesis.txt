Below is a research-based synthesis of the six strategic questions that WFP (or a national nutrition authority) could answer using the Dominican Republic's ENGIH 2018 household income and expenditure survey. The ENGIH is a household consumption and expenditure survey (HCES), and there is now a well-established analytical toolkit—validated against individual-level dietary data—for converting such household food-acquisition data into estimates of apparent nutrient intakes, prevalence of inadequate intake, fortification vehicle coverage, and the expected impact of fortification scenarios [1,4,12,13].

1. Which micronutrients present the greatest risk of inadequate intake?
Analyses of HCES data in comparable settings consistently rank iron, zinc, folate, vitamin A, and calcium as the micronutrients with the highest prevalence of inadequate intake. In the Bangladesh HCES validation study, household-survey–based estimates placed the prevalence of inadequacy at roughly 98% for iron, 99% for calcium, 90% for vitamin A, and 62% for zinc [1]. A systematic review of women's diets in resource-poor settings (including Latin America) found that iron, folate, and zinc were most often below the Estimated Average Requirement (EAR), with folate below the EAR in up to 91% of studies; in Latin America specifically, vitamin A and vitamin C intakes generally exceeded the EAR, but iron, zinc, and B-vitamins (thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, B-6, B-12) remained problematic [8].

For the Dominican Republic specifically, the available national evidence points in the same direction: a 2009 national micronutrient study found vitamin A deficiency (serum retinol <20 µg/dL) in 11.7% of children 6–59 months—a moderate public health problem—and anemia in 28.1% of children 6–59 months, with anemia being the most concrete indicator of iron deficiency in the country [6]. The meta-analysis for Latin America and the Caribbean estimated ~33% anemia prevalence in preschool children, with the Latin Caribbean subregion carrying among the highest burdens [7]. Folate is a particular concern for women of reproductive age in the region because wheat flour in the DR has historically been fortified with iron and B-vitamins but not folic acid [10], leaving neural-tube-defect prevention dependent on supplementation. Calcium, although less commonly prioritized, is almost universally inadequate in cereal-based diets in HCES analyses [1]. The ENGIH 2018 analysis should therefore be expected to identify iron, zinc, folate, vitamin A (especially among children), and calcium as the priority "problem nutrients," with B-vitamins as a secondary concern.

2. Which population groups are most vulnerable?
Young children (especially 6–23 months and <5 years): highest physiological requirements per kg, monotonous complementary diets, and the highest anemia and vitamin A deficiency prevalence [6–8]. The HCES method itself is least reliable for children under 3 years, so their intake gaps may be underestimated unless special attention is given to milk and other child-specific foods [1].
Women of reproductive age, particularly pregnant and lactating women: higher requirements for iron, folate, zinc, and vitamin A that are rarely met by diets in resource-poor settings; pregnant women showed a greater share of studies with intakes below the EAR [8]. Folate adequacy in this group is also critical for NTD prevention [10].
Poor, rural, and low-education households: in the DR, receipt of iron and vitamin A supplements and deworming was strongly associated with maternal education and family wealth rather than with child need, meaning the poorest children were least likely to receive these interventions [6]. Anemia is systematically higher in low-socioeconomic-status and rural children across the region [7].
School-age children in low-SES settings: often neglected because surveys focus on under-5s, yet they still showed elevated anemia (~26%) when poor [7].
Household surveys also reveal that intra-household allocation is not equitable: male adults often meet energy needs while women and children in the same household do not, which the adult-male-equivalent (AME) allocation method only partially captures [1].

3. Which foods contribute the greatest proportion of key micronutrients?
The ENGIH 2018 can answer this directly by computing the contribution of each food item to total household micronutrient supply (e.g., iron, zinc, vitamin A, folate, calcium). Based on regional evidence, the expected "heavy contributors" in the Dominican diet include:

Rice, beans/legumes, and plantain as energy and iron/zinc sources (with beans also contributing folate and zinc, albeit with limited iron bioavailability).
Wheat flour and bread (pan de agua, sobao) as vehicles already carrying iron, thiamin, riboflavin, and niacin; in the DR, per-capita wheat-flour consumption (~65 g/day from FAO food balance sheets) is higher than in Central America, making wheat products a potentially meaningful contributor to B-vitamin and iron intakes [10].
Sugar as a candidate vitamin A vehicle (fortification planned but reportedly not implemented as of the 2009 micronutrient study [6]); in Guatemala, sugar fortification delivered ~350 RE/day to young children and sharply reduced vitamin A deficiency [2].
Edible oil and salt, which in other national assessments proved to be the vehicles delivering the largest shares of vitamin A and iodine, respectively [9,11].
Milk (liquid and powdered), which in Costa Rica was deliberately fortified because it reaches young children and poor rural households in ways that flour cannot [2].
Methodologically, the HCES approach converts purchases of wheat-flour-containing products (breads, pasta, biscuits) into "fortified wheat-flour equivalents" using standard flour-content factors, and then attributes nutrient contributions to households [4]. In the DR, a similar approach would be applied to rice, maize flour, oil, sugar, and salt.

4. What coverage do the main fortification vehicles have?
ENGIH 2018 can estimate both reach (percentage of households acquiring each fortifiable food) and quantity consumed per adult equivalent, which together define effective coverage [4,13]. The relevant baseline knowledge for the DR is:

Wheat flour: mandatory fortification with iron (30 ppm), thiamin, riboflavin, and niacin has been in place since 1992 [10]. Coverage depends on the share of households buying industrially milled flour and bakery products, which is expected to be high in urban areas but lower in rural areas where rice, beans, and home-produced foods dominate.
Salt iodization: generally high household availability in Latin America; the ENGIH can quantify purchase frequency and the share of iodized vs. non-iodized salt, as done in Côte d'Ivoire where ~86–95% of salt samples were iodized [11].
Vitamin A fortification: sugar fortification had been planned but was not operational at the time of the 2009 national study; vitamin A was being added to cornmeal [6]. This means the DR lacks the "universal vehicle" that made sugar fortification successful in Guatemala [2].
Maize flour and rice: coverage depends on the degree of industrial processing; in Latin America, most countries have legislation for wheat flour, maize flour, and/or rice fortification, and the region's centralized milling industry favors fortification feasibility [2].
The key point is that coverage measured as "households that purchase a fortifiable food" is not the same as effective coverage ("households that receive meaningful nutrient amounts"), because quantities consumed and fortification levels also matter [4,11,13].

5. Which groups fall outside the reach of current interventions?
The clearest lesson from the region is that mass fortification of wheat flour systematically misses the poorest, most deficient populations. In Guatemala, the poorest households purchased only ~7 g/day of fortified-wheat-flour equivalents versus 110 g/day among non-poor households; wheat flour fortification thus delivered only ~1% of the iron EAR to the poorest women versus ~12% to the non-poor, and 5% vs. 71% of the folate EAR [3]. By analogy, in the DR the likely under-reached groups are:

Rural and poor households whose diets center on rice, beans, plantain, and home-produced foods rather than industrially processed wheat products [3,13].
Children 6–23 months, who consume small amounts of family staples and whose nutrient needs cannot be met through flour fortification alone; in Costa Rica this was addressed by fortifying milk [2].
Households headed by less-educated mothers, who in the DR already show lower receipt of iron and vitamin A supplements [6].
Migrant and informal communities (e.g., Haitian migrant populations), which face additional barriers documented for DR school feeding and supplementation contexts [7].
Women of reproductive age needing folic acid, who are currently unprotected by fortification since DR wheat flour standards do not include folic acid [10].
HCES data are well suited to mapping these gaps: they can characterize non-consuming households by income quintile, region, and household composition, and identify exactly where supplementation or targeted programs must complement fortification [13].

6. What would be the potential contribution of different fortification scenarios?
The standard approach—fully applicable to ENGIH 2018—is to (a) estimate baseline usual nutrient intakes using AME-based allocation and EAR cut-point/probability methods; (b) simulate the addition of specified fortificant levels to candidate vehicles; and (c) estimate the resulting reduction in the prevalence of inadequate intake and the proportion of the EAR delivered, while checking the tolerable upper intake level (UL) to avoid excess [1,4,12,13]. Evidence on what such scenarios can achieve:

Vitamin A fortification of sugar (as in Guatemala) is one of the best-documented successes in the region: sugar delivered ~350 RE/day to children under 6 years, cutting vitamin A deficiency from 22% to 9% in children, and is highly cost-effective (~US$0.07 per person-year at the time) [2].
Wheat flour with folic acid (as in Chile) reduced neural tube defects by ~43% at very low cost, with cost savings exceeding program costs by ~12:1 [2]. Adding folic acid to DR wheat flour would be the logical scenario to close the current folate gap [10].
Iron via flour has more limited potential: even with good compliance, wheat flour in Guatemala delivered only ~2–12% of the iron EAR for women, because consumption is too low and bioavailability is poor in cereal-based diets [3]. Simulations in Côte d'Ivoire showed fortified flour added only ~13–19% of the iron RNI, insufficient for children [11].
Multiple vehicles vs. single vehicles: modeling in Bangladesh showed that oil, sugar, and wheat flour fortification ranked identically for women, but only individual-level data (not HCES) revealed that sugar and wheat flour products are consumed disproportionately by young children [12]. In Cameroon, modeling showed that once oil and flour fortification work well, additional vehicles (biscuits) add almost nothing—underscoring the need to model combinations of vehicles and avoid both redundancy and UL exceedance [5]. In four African countries, measured contributions from fortified foods were large for vitamin A (20–125% of EAR) and iodine (88–253% of EAR) but negligible for iron (0–13% of RNI) when compliance was poor [9].
Rice fortification is a relevant scenario for the DR given rice's centrality in the national diet, though its impact depends on the share of industrially processed rice and on achievable fortificant levels.
In sum, the ENGIH 2018 analysis would most plausibly show: (i) large potential gains from adding folic acid to wheat flour and implementing vitamin A fortification of sugar (and/or oil); (ii) meaningful but incomplete gains from iron fortification of wheat/maize flour, requiring either higher fortificant levels, better bioavailability, or complementary vehicles (e.g., milk, rice); and (iii) the need to monitor iodine from salt and to assess calcium and zinc options, which are often neglected but widely inadequate [1,2,8,9].

Methodological caveats
ENGIH 2018 measures household food acquisition, not individual consumption. Estimates are therefore "apparent intakes," assuming no waste, gifting, or stockpiling, and that food is distributed within the household in proportion to energy requirements (AME method) [1,4,13]. Validations show these assumptions are reasonable at the population level for ages >3 years but can misclassify infants and young children, especially for foods like milk [1]. Comparisons of HCES with observed-weighed food records show that while HCES and individual surveys often agree on vehicle ranking and recommended fortification levels, HCES can miss differences in intra-household coverage (e.g., children's consumption of sugar and wheat products) [12]. For the most vulnerable groups, the ENGIH analysis should therefore be complemented with targeted 24-hour recall data and, where possible, biochemical status data (e.g., the national micronutrient survey) [1,12].
